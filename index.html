What "runs on your device" actually means

It's not marketing language. Here's the mechanical truth of how these pages work.

Your file never moves

When you drop a photo in, your browser reads the bytes straight off your disk into the page's memory. There is no endpoint it could be sent to.

The parser is the page

EXIF and URL parsing are written as plain JavaScript embedded in the file you loaded. The analysis is arithmetic on data already in front of you.

Close the tab, it's gone

Nothing is written to a database because there is no database. No account, no history, no analytics profile built from what you checked.

Questions

Is my photo really not uploaded anywhere?

Correct. The metadata reader is JavaScript inside the page you loaded. Your browser reads the file from your own disk and never opens a connection carrying it, because there is no server-side component to receive it.

If you want to verify this yourself, open your browser's Network tab, run a scan, and watch that no request leaves the page.

What is EXIF data, and why should I care?

EXIF is a block of information most phone cameras and many digital cameras write into every photo automatically. It typically records the capture date and time, the device make and model, camera settings, and on phones almost always the GPS coordinates of where you were standing.

It survives email attachments, messaging apps, and most direct uploads — which is how people accidentally publish the location of their home, their child's school, or their daily routine.

Do screenshots still contain this data?

Usually not. A screenshot re-renders the pixels into a new image and normally drops the original EXIF block, GPS included. That's the quickest safe way to share a photo — though you can also use the strip function in the photo tool.

How accurate is the link checker?

It's a static heuristic analyser: it inspects the URL you give it and flags structural signals of phishing — punycode obfuscation, brand impersonation, hidden credentials, raw IP hosts, and so on. It does not visit the destination or scan the page's contents.

That's a deliberate privacy trade-off, and it's very good at catching the mechanical tricks phishers rely on. Treat a LOW result as "no structural red flags," not a guarantee of safety.

Why only three tools?

Because a tool that does one thing well is worth more than a suite that does ten things adequately. Each of these answers a question people actually search for, in a single interaction, without an account in the way.

Can I use these on my phone?

Yes. Every tool is responsive and works in mobile Safari and Chrome. The photo scanner can read images straight from your camera roll, which is the most useful place to check for GPS leakage.
